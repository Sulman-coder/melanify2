The PET challenge

The estimated annual global PET production exceeds 82 million metric tons (Bhattacharjya et al., 2024). However, current treatment methods such as mechanical recycling, chemical recycling (pyrolysis, glycolysis), and landfilling are too energy-intensive, costly, and environmentally detrimental. Mechanical recycling degrades fiber quality with each cycle ; chemical recycling consumes up to 60% more energy, and it generates greenhouse gas emissions exceeding virgin PET production; and landfilling creates persistent contamination for up to 162+ years (Guo et al., 2025). This highlights the need for an affordable and scalable alternative that doesn’t compromise its environmental benefits. In 2016, Japanese researchers discovered two enzymes: PETase and MHETase (Yoshida et al., 2016). It shows promising capabilities in breaking down PET plastic. However, plastic degradation often operates well at higher temperatures (65-72°C). Below 75°C (glass state), amorphous chains — regions of PET plastic are the only sites where enzymes can degrade the polymer — are rigid and frozen, making enzyme binding slow and inefficient despite being chemically accessible. To overcome these thermal barriers, researchers developed HotPETase. An enzyme that can maintain plastic degradation around 80.5°C (Bell et al., 2022; Avilan et al., 2023). This is crucial because amorphous plastic, ~70% of all PET plastic in consumer products (Arnal et al., 2023; Tournier et al., 2020), begins to soften around 70°C, the glass transition temperature (Tg) of PET (Thomsen et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2025). Yet, a significant limitation remains: HotPETase typically begins to lose activity within 2–3 hours at 60–65°C (Arnal et al., 2023), this is not ideal considering that PET breakdown to >90% conversion takes 10–24 hours depending on enzyme variant and conditions (Tournier et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2025).

Repeated mechanical recycling has been shown to reduce the molecular quality of PET, supporting the development of complementary recovery methods. A recent study found that during repeated extrusion of bottle-grade PET, the intrinsic viscosity fell from approximately 0.80 to 0.65 dL/g, which is a decline of about 19%. It indicates polymer chain degradation under experimental conditions, rather than a universal 19% decline in overall quality. (Strakova et al., 2026). However, repeated chemical recycling does not necessarily cause progressive quality loss. It generally depolymerizes PET polymers to recover building blocks. Through the process of acetolysis at 280°C for two hours, researchers were able to recover TPA at 95.8% yield with >99.7% purity. (Peng et al., 2023). This demonstrates effective monomer recovery, although energy requirements and purification must still be considered when evaluating sustainability. Biological processing offers another route. Some microorganisms have the ability to produce enzymes that hydrolyses PET. The PETase enzyme possesses the ability to break PET into smaller compounds, including MHET where MHET then gets depolymerised even further to release TPA and EG through MHETase. Enzymatic depolymerization and biological upcycling are distinct stages where enzymatic depolymerization breaks down the polymer, while biological upcycling uses engineered biochemical pathways to convert recovered compounds into different products. (Tournier et al., 2020) achieved at least 90% depolymerization within ten hours using an engineered hydrolase under the experimental conditions, demonstrating the potential of enzyme-based recovery. Aside from recovering monomers, Sandler and Wallace (2021) engineered Escherichia coli to convert TPA into vanillin, achieving 79% conversion rate after optimization. They also demonstrated vanillin production from PET-bottle-derived material. Crucially, this percentage describes TPA conversion, not the fraction of an entire bottle

transformed into vanillin. These findings establish a pathway for converting PET-derived carbon into valuable chemicals.

The local context

Despite a reported 88.8% municipal waste collection rate, Thailand generates an estimated 428 kilotons of mismanaged plastic waste annually due to collection gaps and unsanitary disposal facilities [1]. While rural areas produce 70.1% of the waste exposed to environmental wash-off, extreme urban density makes Bangkok a critical leakage point [1]. Within Thailand's high-priority river catchments, just 10 districts in and around Bangkok account for 51.7% of all exposed mismanaged plastic [1]. Massive volumes of this uncollected waste are dumped directly into waterways like the Chao Phraya River [1]. Once in the river system, 47.6% of this plastic inevitably flows into the ocean, resulting in an average marine discharge of 9.3 kilotons annually [1]. This figure surges to 14.3 kilotons during the rainy season, heavily contaminating vulnerable coastal ecosystems [1].

Thailand endures severe year-round solar radiation, with the national UV index averaging 11 and 12 annually and surging to extreme levels between 13 and 14 across the country during the March hot season [2, 4]. Across major provinces like Bangkok and Phuket, a mere 15 to 20 minutes of midday exposure triggers acute sunburn [4]. The World Health Organization classifies this intense ultraviolet radiation as a direct carcinogen that damages cellular DNA, inflames skin, and weakens immunity [3]. Over time, chronic exposure leads to severe health conditions, including cataracts, premature skin aging, and life-threatening skin cancers [3]. Because global health guidelines mandate sun protection at a UV index of 3 or above [3], Thailand’s extreme solar radiation levels make daily broad-spectrum sunscreen application an essential health requirement for millions of outdoor workers and residents.

However, commercial sunscreens rely heavily on synthetic UV filters like Benzophenone-3 (oxybenzone) that function as severe marine contaminants [6]. Once washed into coastal waters, oxybenzone becomes highly toxic to marine life, especially under direct sunlight [6]. At concentrations as low as 6.5 µg/L, it severely deforms coral larvae by damaging their DNA and forcing them to prematurely develop skeletons, which traps and kills the larvae [6]. This directly triggers widespread coral bleaching, which became so significant that in August 2021, Thailand legally banned sunscreens containing oxybenzone, octinoxate, 4-methylbenzylidene camphor, and butylparaben across all marine national parks [5]. This ban exposes a critical gap in technology: people still need daily protection from intense UV radiation, but the environment can no longer survive the chemical runoff required to provide it.

The proposed pathway

MELANIFY is designed to convert PET plastic waste into allomelanin through a multi-step biological pathway. First, engineered Pichia pastoris produces and secretes HotPETase, which begins degrading PET. Surface-displayed MHETase in E. coli then further breaks down the PET-derived intermediate MHET into terephthalic acid (TPA). The TPA is subsequently fed into an engineered E. coli pathway containing tphA1, tphA2, tphA3, and tphB, which converts TPA into protocatechuate (PCA).

From there, MELANIFY converts PCA into the melanin precursor catechol using AroY, with EcdB synthesizing the prenylated-FMN (prFMN) cofactor and EcdD supporting AroY activity and cofactor maturation. Finally, catechol undergoes oxidation and polymerization under open-air, slightly basic conditions with small amounts of CuSO₄, forming catechol-melanin/allomelanin. In short, the core concept is PET → MHET → TPA → PCA → catechol → allomelanin, turning a persistent plastic waste stream into a functional biomaterial.
