HotPETase

↻Cycle 1DESIGN / BUILD / TEST / LEARN

01Design

There are many ways in which PETase can be modified to increase its thermostability. N-linked glycosylation is one of the countless modifications that can do just that. Pichia pastoris is the perfect host for this because it can rapidly produce enzymes at high yields (>20 g/L with optimized fermentation) while simultaneously glycosylating them through its endoplasmic reticulum post-translational modification machinery (Deng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025).

We aimed to improve catalytic efficiency by choosing Pichia Pastoris as a host for PETase.

02Build

Screened the wild-type PETase sequence (PDB: 6EQE) to predict potential glycosylation sites using highly accurate bioinformatics servers. N-linked glycosylation sites were identified using NetNGlyc - 1.0, while O-linked glycosylation sites were predicted utilizing NetOGlyc - 4.0.

Comparative Modeling: Prepared two distinct systems for a head-to-head comparison: the Wild-type PETase (Control) and the Glycosylated PETase.

03Test

Environment Setup: Solvated both protein structures in a rectangular water box and neutralized the systems by adding 0.15 M NaCl ions.

Heat Stress Simulation: Strictly configured the simulation temperature to 343.15 K (70°C) across all parameter files to mimic a high-heat industrial plastic degradation environment.

Hardware & Execution: Deployed GPU-accelerated OpenMM on a remote Linux server, utilizing tmux and automated Bash scripting for uninterrupted background processing.

Equilibration: Performed initial energy minimization and thermodynamic equilibration to relax the molecular structures and reach the target temperature and pressure.

Production Run: Executed a continuous 10-nanosecond (10 ns) production run, generating detailed .dcdstructural trajectories for both the control and the glycosylated systems.

Data Processing: Processed the trajectory files using Python, specifically leveraging the MDTraj, Pandas, and Matplotlib libraries.

RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation): Calculated backbone RMSD to evaluate overall structural stability and identify unfolding events over the 10 ns timeframe.

RMSF (Root Mean Square Fluctuation): Computed C-alpha RMSF to pinpoint per-residue flexibility, demonstrating how the glycan shield physically restricts the movement of highly flexible loops.

SASA (Solvent Accessible Surface Area): Measured the protein's surface area to assess the degree of structural swelling, core compaction, and solvent exposure upon severe heat stress.

04Learn

1. Backbone RMSD Reveals Enhanced Global Thermostability

As shown in the RMSD analysis, the wild-type PETase (Control, 6EQE) maintained relative stability for the first 6 ns but exhibited a sharp, sudden spike in RMSD at approximately 7 ns (increasing from ~1.0 Å to ~1.7 Å). This abrupt deviation strongly indicates the onset of thermal unfolding and loss of native structure. In contrast, the glycosylated PETase demonstrated an initial adjustment phase (likely accommodating the bulky glycan structures) followed by a highly stable plateau for the remainder of the simulation. These results suggest that the introduction of N-linked glycans effectively prevents the rapid heat-induced structural degradation observed in the wild-type enzyme.

2. C-alpha RMSF Highlights Local Rigidification by Glycan Shields

The wild-type PETase displayed high degrees of flexibility, characterized by prominent fluctuation peaks at specific loop regions, most notably around residues 30-50 and 200-250. Remarkably, the glycosylated PETase exhibited a significant suppression of these fluctuations across the entire protein sequence. The local flexibility in the aforementioned highly mobile loops was markedly reduced. This damping effect indicates that the engineered glycan networks act as physical anchors, restricting excessive thermal motion and rigidifying the surface loops, which is a crucial mechanism for maintaining enzymatic stability at elevated temperatures.

3. SASA Analysis Indicates Preservation of Structural Core

The wild-type PETase exhibited severe fluctuations in total SASA, culminating in a sharp, abnormal drop between 9.5 and 10 ns. This drastic reduction in surface area perfectly correlates with the unfolding event observed in the RMSD data, suggesting a structural collapse or abnormal hydrophobic core packing due to thermal denaturation. Conversely, the glycosylated PETase maintained a much more consistent and steady SASA profile throughout the 10 ns simulation. This consistency further confirms that the glycan shield successfully protects the structural core of the enzyme, preventing the fatal structural collapse that occurs in the unglycosylated wild-type under identical heat stress conditions.

↓ Carry strategy to a stronger scaffold

↻Cycle 2DESIGN / BUILD / TEST / LEARN

01Design

Cycle 1 suggested that N-linked glycosylation could improve PETase stability at elevated temperatures. Rather than continuing to optimize the weaker wild-type scaffold, we transferred the strategy to HotPETase (PDB: 7QVH), a thermostable PETase scaffold, to determine whether glycosylation could further stabilize an already heat-resistant enzyme.

02Build

Screened the HotPETase sequence (PDB: 7QVH) to predict potential glycosylation sites using highly accurate bioinformatics servers. N-linked glycosylation sites were identified using NetNGlyc - 1.0, while O-linked glycosylation sites were predicted utilizing NetOGlyc - 4.0.

Comparative Modeling: Prepared two distinct systems for a head-to-head comparison: the Wild-type HotPETase (Control) and the Glycosylated HotPETase.

03Test

Instead of repeating the full Cycle 1 analysis, we focused on structural compatibility and active-site preservation:

● Compared the recombinant HotPETase construct with the experimentally determined 7QVH structure. ● Mapped predicted glycosylation sites relative to the catalytic triad (Ser160, Asp206, His237). ● Simulated glycosylated and non-glycosylated HotPETase for 10 ns at 70°C. ● Evaluated global stability and local flexibility.

04Learn

Structural Superposition & Tag Influence: We modeled the recombinant construct (with the N-terminal extension and C-terminal tag)(cyan) and superimposed it with the wild-type HotPETase (PDB ID: 7QVH) (green). The alignment yielded an exceptionally low Global RMSD of 0.233 Å. As shown in the attached image, the core fold remains entirely undisrupted, and the terminal extensions are fully solvent-exposed, projecting away from the main structure.

N-Glycosylation Site Prediction & Active Site Analysis: Based on our FASTA sequence numbering, we identified 5 predicted N-glycosylation sites at positions: 114, 138, 190, 264, and 277. We mapped these onto the 3D structure (see attached image highlighting the catalytic triad: Ser160, Asp206, His237 in red). We noted that positions N190 and N264 are in close structural proximity to the catalytic triad residues.

However, based on our previous 10ns Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations comparing the unglycosylated and glycosylated forms, the addition of glycans at these sites did not induce steric hindrance in the active site pocket. Instead, the glycosylated model demonstrated significantly improved structural stability and lower RMSF values, suggesting these glycans act as stabilizing anchors suitable for high-temperature conditions.

Overall, our results indicate that HotPETase structural stability at 70°C is significantly enhanced by N-linked glycosylation. Glycosylated Enzyme (Blue Line): Maintains a consistently low Backbone RMSD (~1.0–1.5 Å) across the entire 10 ns simulation, demonstrating strong structural stability at 343 K (70°C). Non-Glycosylated Enzyme (Red Line): Experiences a sharp increase in RMSD after ~2 ns, fluctuating high up to ~4.0 Å, which indicates significant structural degradation/unfolding at high temperature.

01Design

To enable extracellular production and glycosylation of HotPETase, the HotPETase coding sequence was codon-optimized for Pichia pastoris and designed with an N-terminal secretion signal.

02Build

The codon-optimized HotPETase construct was first assembled into pPIC9K and transformed into E. coli DH5α for plasmid propagation. Candidate colonies were screened by colony PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis, with the expected 8,099 bp construct used as the basis for identifying positive clones. Following plasmid extraction, the construct was verified by gel electrophoresis and prepared for P. pastoris transformation.

The plasmid was subsequently linearized with SacI to facilitate genomic integration into P. pastoris. Initial attempts on linearizing the plasmid were largely unsuccessful. This required us to repeat digestion and gel-based purification multiple times. These iterations resulted in substantial plasmid loss, prompting us to return to freshly extracted plasmid. Complete linearization was eventually confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis, after which the purified plasmid was concentrated and prepared for yeast transformation.

The linearized HotPETase construct was added into P. pastoris via electroporation. Then we selected the transformants on a His4 Plate, and colonies were transferred through G418 selection and screening steps to obtain candidate recombinant yeast strains. Successful transformants were expanded in YPD medium before being transferred to BMGY for biomass generation and subsequently BMMY for methanol-induced protein expression.
