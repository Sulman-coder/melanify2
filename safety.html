Safety in the Lab

All laboratory work followed the standards maintained by SHECU, the university’s central body for safety, occupational health and environment. Before bench access, every team member completed the required safety training covering chemical handling, biological agents, personal protective equipment, waste segregation, and emergency response.

Inside the laboratory we wore safety glasses or goggles and fully buttoned lab coats at all times, tied back long hair, and selected gloves rated for the specific chemical being handled, removing them and washing hands before leaving the room. Eating, drinking and smoking were prohibited, we did not work alone, and exit doors were never locked.

Our host laboratory is assessed against the ESPReL checklist, the national laboratory safety framework covering safety policy and responsibility, chemical inventory, waste management, laboratory design and equipment, emergency preparedness and risk management, personnel training, and document control, with findings used to correct weak areas.

Chemical Safety

Since the AOX1 promoter that drives HotPETase is methanol inducible, methanol plays a pivotal role in our Pichia research. All induction cultures were handled in a chemical fume hood or biosafety cabinet because methanol is flammable and highly hazardous by inhalation, ingestion, and skin absorption. We also kept stock bottles in a flammables cabinet and never utilized volumes more than necessary for the induction schedule.

Extraction with phenol and chloroform was necessary for plasmid linearization in yeast transformation. All organic waste was collected separately as halogenated and non-halogenated streams instead of being poured to the sewer. Both substances were handled entirely in a fume hood. Phenol causes severe burns, which can initially be painless.

In addition to the aromatic intermediates of our own route, other chemical risks include the hazardous multicopy selection agent G418 (Geneticin), which necessitates dust control and gloves during weighing, antibiotics and IPTG for E. coli induction and selection, flammable ethanol and isopropanol, and other substances. Because of the potential for skin absorption and sensitization, terephthalic acid, protocatechuic acid, and catechol were all handled in the fume hood; the latter two were particularly targeted as systemic toxicants and irritants.

For the high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) examination of these three analytes, mobile phases made of acetonitrile and acidified solutions were generated in the hood and then discarded as solvent waste. Copper sulfate, a heavy metal waste product, was never sent down the drain after its usage in our allomelanin autoxidation stage. Chemicals were organized according to their danger classes, with incompatibles kept in separate containers, acids stored at a safe distance from bases and below eye level, and no chemicals were ever housed in a fume hood. The labels on all containers were carefully preserved and could be read.

Equipment & Physical Safety

We dealt with chemical dangers as well as physical ones. We made sure to inspect the cuvettes for any signs of cracks or arcing before each kilovolt pulse used for electroporation of P. pastoris, and we discharged the unit before handling it.

Running the probe sonicator in cycles that kept samples on ice and inside an enclosure with hearing protection was necessary since it produced both aerosols from lysate and hearing damage. After the autoclave pressure had returned to atmospheric, the loads were handled with heat-resistant gloves.

When imaging gel, the UV transilluminator was operated with the shield in place. When removing gel, a face shield was also worn. Our project hardware is a UV-C bench instrument, and we have taken precautions to ensure that no one’s skin or eyes will be exposed to UV-C while it is in operation by placing the source inside an interlocking enclosure.

Safety in Human Practices

As part of our initiative, we polled stakeholders, professionals, and the general public for their thoughts. For their privacy’s sake, we made sure to get their consent before publishing their names on our wiki, presentation materials, etc. We only know that they stopped by our booth to take the survey, since we kept all responses to large-scale surveys anonymous. Their responses were fully anonymised, and once they were submitted, we lost all access to any personal information they may have provided.

Protecting our participants and ensuring the validity of our research are both aided by approaching Human Practices with care following the processes mentioned above.

Safety in Education

In order to familiarize children with synthetic biology technology, we organized hands-on laboratory workshops. These seminars also help kids who are thinking about working in a lab learn the basics of safety in the lab and how to conduct experiments correctly from a young age.

Every session started with a thorough evaluation of potential risks, and the experimental protocol provided detailed instructions on how to lessen such risks. Each session began with a review of the protocols by our biology teacher, lab assistant, and principal to ensure the safety of the participants.

To make sure that kids could safely investigate scientific concepts under supervision, we chose materials that were easy for them to obtain and did not pose too much of a risk. Students watched the reaction without touching the reagent as certain members of our team handled the hydrogen peroxide during our elephant toothpaste demonstration.

Safety in Real-World Application

CURRENT DEVELOPMENT STAGE

We have not yet manufactured the final sunscreen formulation or conducted experiments to confirm its safety for human usage because our project is still in the development phase.

Our long-term goal is to have MELANIFY serve as a platform that allows users to convert their plastic trash into sunscreen made from melanin. We spoke with recycling industry insiders to assess the potential for using plastic-derived feedstocks in our manufacturing process and to learn about the logistical challenges that could arise from implementing this strategy. To help us understand the obstacles and safety measures needed to turn our research into a marketable product, we consulted with industry professionals.

Our formulation needs to be thoroughly tested to ensure it is safe, stable, and effective against UV radiation. We must also ensure that it complies with all applicable regulations before it can be used in the real world.
